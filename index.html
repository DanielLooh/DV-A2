Daniel Loh · 13 November 2026

1

How Australia pays now

The big shift happened fast — and it is mostly a story about cards.

The Reserve Bank surveys how Australians pay roughly every three years. Two decades ago, cash carried most payments by number. In 2025 it carried about one in seven, and cards — debit above all — carry most of the rest.

Read the chart: the thickness of each band shows its share of payments by number, rescaled so the rounded source shares sum to 100% in each survey year. The vertical position has no meaning. The cash band collapses after 2007 and stabilises near 15% in the 2020s. Bank transfers and other methods account for smaller shares.

2

Phones take over the tap

In-person card payments are moving from plastic to phone.

A card payment at the counter used to mean a plastic card. Since 2022, a growing share happens with a phone or watch instead — roughly 40% of in-person card payments by 2025.

Read the chart: each grid contains 100 squares. Rounded channel shares are rescaled to fill the grid; the 2022 squares represent about 1 percentage point each. Labels retain the published shares. Device tapping grows while inserting a card stays at about 5%.

Online, the same pattern holds: payments keep growing and nearly half now happen inside a phone app.

Read the chart: the top panel stacks the ways people pay online. The bottom panel tracks the share of online payments made in a mobile app, which reached about 46% in 2025.

3

Who still uses cash

Cash has retreated purpose by purpose — and the people still relying on it are not evenly distributed.

Cash use fell across the purposes shown, with different starting points and rates of decline. These survey snapshots describe the change; they do not explain why people changed payment methods.

Read the chart: paired bars compare cash shares in 2007 (slate) and 2025 (magenta). The top panel groups in-person payments by purpose; the bottom panel compares all payments by number and by value. Leisure and groceries/fuel have no 2007 figure in the supplied data, so only their 2025 bars are shown.

If payments keep going digital, who is at risk of being left behind? The digital-inclusion data gives a clear answer: older Australians, low-income households and public housing residents.

Read the chart: each dot is the share of a group who are highly digitally excluded. The most-affected groups sit at three times the national average.

Card use itself spans every age and income group — but the type of card differs. Debit dominates everywhere; credit skews strongly to higher incomes.

Read the chart: the top pair compares card use across age and income. The bottom pair splits that card use into debit and credit by income quartile.

4

Where cash is practical

Digital inclusion and access to cash describe different parts of the payment landscape.

Digital inclusion combines access, affordability and digital ability. State and territory averages differ, and can conceal larger gaps within communities.

Read the map: darker blue means a higher digital-inclusion score. The ACT leads; every state sits within about ten points.

Bank branch counts have fallen in every state and territory since 2017. Bank@Post, the banking services offered through participating post offices, provides another way to bank in person. The map shows where these locations are recorded; it does not show whether they replace a closed branch.

Read the map: each equal-sized dot marks one Bank@Post location in June 2026. The insets enlarge Sydney and Melbourne, where dots overlap. Dots show locations, not the number of customers or services available.

Branches are only one channel. Every way of reaching a bank in person has shrunk since 2017 — except Bank@Post, the banking services offered through post offices.

Read the chart: each tile is a state's count of that service, indexed to 100 in June 2017. Pink means decline, blue means growth. Bank@Post is the only column that holds steady or grows in most states.

Access to cash varies sharply by remoteness. In very remote areas, 95% of residents live within about 95 km of a cash point; in major cities it is under 2 km.

Read the chart: the top panel shows how far 95% of residents live from their nearest way to withdraw cash, by remoteness. The bottom panel compares service types — post offices keep most people within about 4 km.

And digital payments need a connection. Outside the cities, interruptions to mobile and internet service are a routine part of life — one reason cash remains a practical backup.

Read the chart: each bar shows how often people in each remoteness area report interruptions to their mobile or internet use.

Compare the branch network in 2017 and 2026. Nearby locations are grouped into geographic cells; the map shows the network in each year rather than tracking individual closures.

Read the map: circle area is proportional to the number of bank branches in that area. The radio buttons flip between years.

Put the whole picture together and a pattern emerges: digitally ready states are still losing their in-person banking services. Bank@Post provides another face-to-face channel. These state totals cannot establish whether it is the last service in an individual community.

Read the chart: each line is a state, traced across digital-inclusion scores and the change in each in-person channel. Each axis is scaled separately from its lowest to highest state value; higher means a higher score or a smaller service decline. Positions across different axes are not directly comparable. Hover a line to isolate a state.

5

Newer ways to pay

Australians know about PayID and QR codes — far fewer use them.

New payment methods face a gap between awareness and use. Nearly nine in ten Australians know about PayID; far fewer have used it in the past year.

Read the chart: each row compares the share of people aware of a method (blue) with the share who used it in the past 12 months (magenta). Open markers and “under” labels flag figures published only as upper bounds.

Behind the apps are networks that move money between accounts. The New Payments Platform (NPP), which enables fast bank transfers, has the strongest growth in payment value among the services shown; cheque payment values are falling.

Read the chart: bars show year-ended growth in the value of payments on each rail, to September 2025 and September 2026.

Payment rules have also changed. The timeline places cash acceptance, card fees, recipient name checks and the planned end of cheques alongside the introduction of newer payment services. It is a dated snapshot, as at 13 November 2026.

Read the chart: each row is a milestone, placed by date. Blue dots are already in force; red dots are still to come. The dashed line marks 13 November 2026. Year-only and month-only milestones use the start of that period for placement; mid-2027 is approximate.

6

The price of convenience

Scams and card fraud measure different risks and should be read separately.

The scam categories shown have different loss trends. Separately, the reported domestic fraud rate for card payments made without presenting the card fell between the years shown. These are different measures; neither establishes that digital payments caused a change in risk.

Read the chart: the top panel compares reported scam losses by type, 2024 vs 2025 (log scale). The bottom panel shows the domestic card-not-present fraud rate falling from about $1.07 to about 54 cents per $1,000 spent.

■

What it adds up to

Cash is becoming a backup — but a backup still needs somewhere to be withdrawn.

Australia’s payment habits have changed within a generation: cash fell from 69% of payments in 2007 to 15% in 2025. Cards dominate payment counts, and devices account for a growing share of card payments.

The transition is uneven. Digital exclusion is higher in some groups, cash withdrawal distances are greater in remote areas, and bank branch counts have fallen. A smaller cash share does not remove the need to consider cash access and reliable alternatives.

Data notes

  • Survey precision. The RBA Consumer Payments Survey covers roughly 1,200 people and 14,000 transactions; no confidence intervals are published, so small differences between groups should be read with caution.
  • Approximate figures. Values marked “~” are taken from rounded figures in RBA text or chart descriptions rather than underlying data. Upper bounds are labelled “under”; lower bounds are labelled “over”. The 2007–2025 survey series runs roughly every three years, so lines between survey years interpolate rather than observe.
  • Conflicting figures excluded. Convenient cash access (75% vs 78%), the 2025 total card-fraud value, the share of over-65s paying entirely in cash, registered PayID counts and BNPL usage each had conflicting or unverified published figures and were left out of charts. Confirmed figures will replace them.
  • ATMs are not all disappearing. The fall in bank-operated ATMs partly reflects banks selling ATM fleets to independent operators, not only closures. Independent ATMs are counted separately.
  • Branch counts. APRA's totals are 5,694 (June 2017) and 3,108 (June 2026); a small number of records lack coordinates, so the symbol map plots 5,690 and 3,106.
  • No per-capita figures. APRA's published population figures only cover SA2s with services, so this page avoids per-capita comparisons until ABS population data is added.
  • Correlation, not causation. This page describes associations in published data; it does not claim causal relationships between payment behaviour, access or exclusion.
  • Coverage and exclusions. No regional payment-method dataset or LGA-level digital-inclusion data is included: maps describe access and readiness, not regional payment behaviour. APRA EFTPOS counts and an unverified credit interchange-cap comparison are excluded. Irregular monthly retail-payment levels are not drawn as a continuous series.
  • Verification. The September 2026 growth-table columns were checked against the RBA release. The buy now pay later licensing date was checked against ASIC. Remaining secondary fraud figures are recorded in the hand-off notes; map boundaries have been replaced with official ABS data.
  • Scam-loss data. Loss figures combine several reporting bodies and cover reported losses only — actual losses are likely higher. The supplied CSV has no 2025 investment-scam value; that category is omitted from the comparison.
